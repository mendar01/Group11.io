Financial Inequality Slide 2 of 8 · Definitions
Speaker 1

Three words we must not confuse

Income inequality

Unequal distribution of what people earn — wages, salaries, benefits — usually measured over a year.

Wealth inequality

Unequal distribution of what people own — property, savings, shares — accumulated over a lifetime and inherited.

Economic inequality

The umbrella term covering both, plus gaps in opportunity: education, healthcare and social mobility.

How economists measure the gap

  • Gini coefficient — a single number from 0 (everyone equal) to 1 (one person takes everything). The OECD average is about 0.31.
  • Share of total — for example, the percentage of national income taken by the richest 10%.
  • Wealth per adult — the stock of assets each person holds, not the flow of income.

Our key vocabulary today

wealth concentration the top 1% bottom 50% Gini coefficient wage stagnation social mobility redistribution disposable income the wealth gap

Financial Inequality Slide 4 of 8 · Causes
Speaker 3

Why the gap has widened

1. Technological change & globalisation

Automation and offshoring removed many mid-skill manufacturing jobs in rich countries, while returns flowed to those who own capital and to highly skilled workers.

2. Declining bargaining power of labour

Falling trade-union membership and more flexible contracts weakened workers' ability to negotiate pay rises — a pattern economists call wage stagnation.

3. Capital outgrows wages

Returns on shares, property and savings have grown faster than wages, so the gap between owners and earners widens automatically each year.

4. Inheritance and the “birth lottery”

Oxfam estimates that 36% of billionaire wealth is inherited. Wealth bought once keeps earning without further work, and it passes to the next generation largely untaxed in many countries.

Financial Inequality Slide 5 of 8 · Effects
Speaker 3

What inequality does to a society

Economic drag

  • Poor households cannot invest in education or health, so talent is wasted.
  • Demand is concentrated at the top, where people save rather than spend.
  • The IMF's Fiscal Monitor: Tackling Inequality (2017) found that high inequality can undermine the durability of growth.

Social effects

  • Frozen social mobility: your postcode predicts your income.
  • Erosion of trust in institutions, resentment between groups.
  • Widening gaps in life expectancy and mental health.

Political effects

  • Concentration of wealth can translate into political influence.
  • Capture of the rules — tax, competition, labour law.
  • Populism and protest movements on both sides of the debate.

Counter-argument we should address: inequality can reward innovation and effort. The problem is not the existence of rich people — it is wealth concentration so extreme that it prevents others from competing on equal terms. That is what the next slide is about.

Weigh both sides Transition → “So what could actually reduce the gap?”
Financial Inequality Slide 6 of 8 · Solutions
Speaker 4

Four policy levers

1. Progressive taxation

Tax capital gains, property and large inheritances closer to the rates applied to wages. Oxfam's Inequality Inc. (2024) argues that taxing the wealthiest would raise money for public investment; the OECD's Taxation and Inequality (2024) discusses the design of such taxes for G20 finance ministers.

2. Invest in human capital

Public education, childcare and healthcare widen opportunity and make the “social elevator” work again — the central recommendation of the OECD's social-mobility research.

3. Fair wages and bargaining power

Minimum wages indexed to living costs, stronger collective bargaining, and profit-sharing schemes so that employees own a stake in growth.

4. Data and transparency

Trustworthy statistics are themselves a policy tool: the WID.world project and the World Bank's Poverty and Inequality Platform make governments' promises measurable.

Honest limitation: wealth taxes are hard to administer, capital can move, and there is no single policy that closes the gap — only a combination, over decades.

Policy, not charity Each lever: what it does → who it affects → what could go wrong.
Financial Inequality Slide 7 of 8 · Conclusion & discussion
Speaker 4

Where we stand

  • Financial inequality is measurable — the Gini coefficient and top-10% shares turn a moral debate into a testable question.
  • The trend is not uniform. Inequality between countries has fallen since 1990 (global Gini: 70 → 62), while inequality within many countries has risen.
  • It is a choice, not a law of nature. WID.world's conclusion: tax, education and labour policy shape the distribution.
  • Our group's position: the goal is not absolute equality but an economy where effort — not inheritance — decides success.

Questions we expect from the class

Isn't some inequality useful?
Yes — rewards motivate effort. The debate is about the degree and about equal opportunity, not about zero inequality.
Wouldn't wealth taxes just make capital leave?
That is a real risk; the OECD argues for coordinated rules between countries, not isolated taxes.
Is global inequality rising or falling?
Between countries it has fallen since 1990; within many countries it has risen. Both are true at once.
What can students do?
Follow the data, vote, and check a country's policy before repeating a headline: measurement matters.
