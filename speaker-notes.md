## SPEAKER 1 — Slides 1–2

> **Delivery target:** slow pace, strong opening, clear definitions. Make eye contact with the whole room, not just the tutor.

**Slide 1 (40 sec) — script**

> Good morning, everyone. We are Group [—], and I'm [Name]. With me today are [Name], [Name] and [Name].
>
> Our group has been asked to present one type of inequality, and we chose **financial inequality** — by which we mean both income inequality and wealth inequality.
>
> We'll follow a simple structure: first, definitions and measurement; second, the evidence; third, the causes and the effects on society; and finally, the policy responses and our conclusion. We'll take your questions at the end.

*Delivery notes:* hold a 1-second pause after "financial inequality". The four names should be pointed to as you say them.

**Slide 2 (1 min 50 sec) — script**

> Before we look at numbers, we need to be precise about three terms, because they're often confused.
>
> **Income inequality** is the unequal distribution of what people *earn* — wages, salaries, benefits — usually measured over a year.
>
> **Wealth inequality** is the unequal distribution of what people *own* — property, savings, shares. This is far more extreme, because wealth is accumulated and inherited.
>
> **Economic inequality** is the umbrella term for both, and it also includes unequal access to education and healthcare.
>
> Economists usually summarise this with the **Gini coefficient**, a number from zero to one: zero means everyone is equal, one means a single person takes everything. The average across OECD countries is around 0.31.
>
> So the key vocabulary for our presentation is: wealth concentration, the top one per cent, social mobility, and redistribution.
>
> Now, [Name] will show you exactly how large that gap has become.

*Delivery notes:* define "Gini" slowly — /ˈdʒiːni/. On the handover line, step back and gesture to the next speaker — this is one of the criteria.

## SPEAKER 2 — Slide 3

> **Delivery target:** confidence with numbers, pauses after each figure, clear pointing at the charts.

**Script (2 min 30 sec)**

> Thank you, [Name]. Let me put some figures on that.
>
> According to the **World Inequality Report**, the richest ten per cent of the world's population takes **52 per cent** of global income. The poorest half — that's about four billion people — shares **8.5 per cent**. On average, someone in the top ten per cent earns roughly **38 times** more than someone in the bottom half.
>
> The two charts show the same story from two angles. On the left, global income shares. On the right, household wealth in the United States, from the Federal Reserve's 2022 Survey of Consumer Finances. There, the top ten per cent of households holds **67 per cent** of all wealth — while the bottom half holds just **2.5 per cent**.
>
> So this isn't only a story about poor countries. Wealth is concentrated even in the world's richest economies.
>
> A quick caveat: these are averages, and inequality *between* countries has actually fallen since 1990, largely because of growth in China. But within many countries, the gap has widened. Both facts are true at the same time.
>
> So why does this happen? [Name] will explain the causes.

*Delivery notes:* never read a number you haven't already said out loud in rehearsal. Pause two seconds after "2.5 per cent".

## SPEAKER 3 — Slides 4–5

> **Delivery target:** clear cause-and-effect language, well-controlled gestures, balanced tone.

**Slide 4 — Causes (2 min 30 sec)**

> Thank you, [Name]. We've identified four main drivers.
>
> **First, technological change and globalisation.** Automation and offshoring removed many mid-skilled manufacturing jobs in rich countries, while the gains went to those who own capital and to a small number of highly skilled workers.
>
> **Second, the declining bargaining power of labour.** As trade-union membership fell and contracts became more flexible, workers lost the ability to negotiate pay rises. Economists describe the result as **wage stagnation** — pay that stops rising for decades.
>
> **Third, capital outgrows wages.** Returns on shares, property and savings have grown faster than wages, so the distance between owners and earners widens automatically every year.
>
> **Fourth, inheritance.** Oxfam estimates that **36 per cent of billionaire wealth is inherited**. Wealth earned once keeps earning without any further work.
>
> And these causes reinforce each other. Unequal access to schooling leads to unequal skills, which leads to lower-paying jobs, which means the next generation starts from the same position. The OECD calls this a **broken social elevator** — in several countries it takes four to five generations for a low-income family to reach the average income.
>
> But what does this actually do to a society?

**Slide 5 — Effects (2 min 30 sec)**

> There are three areas of impact.
>
> **Economically**, inequality is a drag. Poor households can't invest in education or health, so talent is wasted; demand concentrates at the top, where people save rather than spend. The IMF's Fiscal Monitor found that high inequality can undermine the **durability of growth** — so this is not only a moral issue.
>
> **Socially**, mobility freezes. Where you are born increasingly predicts what you will earn. Trust in institutions erodes, and gaps in health and life expectancy widen.
>
> **Politically**, concentrated wealth can translate into political influence — the rules on tax, competition and labour law start to be written by those who benefit from them.
>
> Now, one honest counter-argument: inequality can reward innovation and effort, and nobody is proposing absolute equality. The problem is **not** that some people are rich. It is that wealth concentration becomes so extreme that others can no longer compete on equal terms.
>
> So what could actually be done? [Name] will take us through the options.

## SPEAKER 4 — Slides 6–7

> **Delivery target:** strong final speaker; summarise rather than repeat; keep energy high.

**Slide 6 — Solutions (3 min)**

> Thank you, [Name]. We think there are four realistic levers.
>
> **One: progressive taxation** — taxing capital gains, property and large inheritances closer to the rates applied to wages. Oxfam's *Inequality Inc.* report argues this would raise money for public investment, and the OECD discusses the design of such taxes for G20 finance ministers.
>
> **Two: investing in human capital** — public education, childcare and healthcare. This is what makes the social elevator work again.
>
> **Three: fair wages and bargaining power** — minimum wages indexed to living costs, stronger collective bargaining, and profit-sharing so employees own a stake in growth.
>
> **Four: data and transparency.** Reliable statistics are themselves a policy tool. The World Inequality Database and the World Bank's Poverty and Inequality Platform make governments' promises measurable.
>
> We should be honest about the limitations. Wealth taxes are difficult to administer, capital can move across borders, and no single measure will close the gap. What the evidence points to is a combination of policies, sustained over decades.

**Slide 7 — Conclusion (1 min 30 sec)**

> To conclude, four points.
>
> **First**, financial inequality is measurable — the Gini coefficient and top-ten-per-cent shares turn a moral debate into a testable question.
>
> **Second**, the trend is not uniform: inequality *between* countries has fallen since 1990, while inequality *within* many countries has risen.
>
> **Third**, it is a choice, not a law of nature. Tax, education and labour policy shape the distribution — the World Inequality Report puts it directly: inequality is a political choice.
>
> **Fourth**, and this is our group's position: the goal is not absolute equality, but an economy where effort — rather than inheritance — decides who succeeds.
>
> Thank you for listening. We're happy to take your questions.

*Delivery notes:* name each speaker of the panel with a gesture when inviting questions. Keep the source slide on screen.

## Q&A preparation (all four speakers answer)

| Likely question | Answer, and who leads |
|-----------------|-----------------------|
| *"Isn't some inequality useful?"* | Yes — rewards motivate effort. The debate is about the degree and about equal opportunity. **Speaker 3.** |
| *"Wouldn't wealth taxes make capital leave?"* | That is a genuine risk; the OECD argues for coordinated rules between countries rather than isolated taxes. **Speaker 4.** |
| *"Is global inequality rising or falling?"* | Both: falling between countries since 1990, rising within many countries. **Speaker 2.** |
| *"Where did you get your figures?"* | Name the institution and the year: World Inequality Report 2022, OECD Society at a Glance 2024, Federal Reserve SCF 2022, Oxfam 2024/2025. **Speaker 2.** |
| *"What about Russia / our country?"* | Be honest: our presentation uses global and OECD data; national data would need a separate source. Offer to look it up. **any speaker.** |

**If you don't know:** *"That's a good question — we didn't find reliable data on that, but we can follow it up."* Never invent a number.

## Delivery checklist (the assessment criteria)

- [ ] **Pronunciation** — drill *Gini*, *inequality*, *wealth*, *percentage* (see vocabulary pack).
- [ ] **Pace** — pause after every key figure. Silence is not a mistake.
- [ ] **Fluency** — rehearse aloud at least three times; do not memorise word-for-word or you will lose fluency.
- [ ] **Volume** — project to the back wall; the last row must hear the numbers.
- [ ] **Smooth transitions** — every handover uses a named speaker and a linking sentence.
- [ ] **Enthusiasm** — vary your intonation on the statistics; flat delivery kills an interesting topic.
- [ ] **Eye contact** — 3–5 seconds per person, sweep the room, never read from the slide.
- [ ] **Posture & gestures** — stand still, open palms, point at the chart rather than wave.
- [ ] **Knowledge** — be able to explain every figure on your own slide in one extra sentence.
